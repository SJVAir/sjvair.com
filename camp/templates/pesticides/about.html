    What this covers

    Pesticide use in the San Joaquin Valley's eight counties as reported to California's Department of Pesticide Regulation (DPR): applications that happened, from Pesticide Use Reporting, and applications planned, from SprayDays notices of intent.

    Covers

    • Agricultural applications on crops
    • Rights of way and public health treatments such as mosquito control
    • Structural and landscape work
    • Food processing plants and commodity fumigation chambers
    • Notices of intent for restricted materials

    Doesn't cover

    • Home and garden use
    • Measurements of the air, water or soil
    • Exact fields: a square mile is the finest location
    • Who was exposed, or how much

    Pesticide Use Reporting (PUR)

    California requires agricultural pesticide applications to be reported to the county agricultural commissioner, who forwards them to the Department of Pesticide Regulation (DPR).

    Each record gives the product, active ingredient, crop or use site, county, one-square-mile section, date, and amount. The commodity list therefore includes the non-crop sites listed above alongside crops. This explorer loads the records for Fresno, Kern, Kings, Madera, Merced, San Joaquin, Stanislaus, and Tulare counties{% if years %} for {{ years.0 }}–{{ years.1 }}{% endif %}.

    SprayDays notices of intent

    Growers must file a notice of intent before applying a California-restricted material. DPR's SprayDays system publishes those notices 48 hours ahead for fumigants and 24 hours ahead for other restricted materials.

    The grower then has up to four days after the scheduled date to begin. Notices cover only restricted materials and list the product and chemical but not the crop. To be notified about notices near an address, sign up with SprayDays.

    A notice is a plan. It is not confirmation that the application happened. Confirmed use arrives later, through PUR.

    What a record is

    A record is what a grower told the county they applied: a report, not a measurement, located to a square mile.

    They are reports, not measurements

    A record says what a grower told the county they applied. Nobody sampled the air or the soil. An error in a filing carries through to every page here, and DPR checks the paperwork rather than re-measuring the field.

    A location is a square mile, not a field

    Applications are reported to a one-square-mile MTRS section. A section's total covers everything reported inside that square mile; it does not say where in it, or how near a home, a school or a property line. The same limit applies to notices of intent.

    Some active ingredients are withheld

    CDPR reports a few as “AI IS CONFIDENTIAL” or “UNKNOWN”. Those pounds are counted in the totals, because they were applied, but they are left out of chemical rankings and chemical counts, where naming them is the point.

    Years and lag

    Notices of intent are current: SprayDays publishes them before the application, and the explorer fetches them once a day.

    Absence of a record is not proof that nothing was applied. Reporting gaps, late filings and corrections all exist, and a section with no use reported is not the same as a section where nothing happened.

    Flagged chemicals

    The scope bar's Flagged chemicals toggle narrows the whole explorer — every count, pounds figure, leaderboard, table and map square — to the active ingredients that appear on at least one of four official lists.

    Prop 65

    California's list of chemicals known to the state to cause cancer, birth defects or other reproductive harm. More

    CARB TAC

    Air pollutants the California Air Resources Board has identified as posing a health hazard. More

    IARC 1, 2A, 2B

    Agents the International Agency for Research on Cancer rates as carcinogenic, probably carcinogenic, or possibly carcinogenic to humans. Group 3 is not included. More

    Restricted

    Active ingredients the state restricts under 3 CCR 6400, which need a county permit and a notice of intent before each application. More

    A chemical only has to be on one of them. A badge on a chemical anywhere in the explorer means it is on one of them.

    The other toggles

    Restricted materialsKeeps only use of restricted products (see below).
    FumigantsKeeps only use of fumigant products (see below).
    Applied by airKeeps only the applications reported as made by aircraft (CDPR’s application method A).
    Why one toggle for four lists

    The first three are health-hazard listings and the last is a regulatory control, but a reader asking whether something is one of the bad ones is asking a single question, and a material the state restricts belongs in the answer.

    Why “flagged” and not “of concern”

    Three of these four lists are what that phrase usually names; flagged is the union of all four.

    How it differs from the Category filter

    The chemicals list's Category filter picks out a single DPR category (carcinogen, reproductive toxin, toxic air contaminant and so on) one at a time, while the toggle is the union of all four lists above and follows you from page to page.

    Listed isn't exposed. Being listed says a chemical is listed, not how much of it reached anyone — see what the units mean.

    How a product’s status is decided

    A product’s own status comes from CDPR’s per-product restricted flag, which applies 3 CCR 6400’s formulation and use exemptions. Our ingredient list decides only for products CDPR’s file doesn’t list.

    Why a restricted list can include chemicals without the badge

    A chemical’s Restricted badge marks the ingredient list, so a list under the Restricted materials toggle can include chemicals without the badge: the co-ingredients of restricted products.

    How these numbers are made

    Everything here is arithmetic on the records described above. Nothing is modeled, estimated or interpolated: if a number appears on this site, a grower filed it.

    What that arithmetic does and does not support:

    What counts as a fumigant

    Fumigants are decided over every loaded year, not one at a time

    A product is a fumigant if CDPR flags it as one, or if it contains a fumigant active ingredient. An ingredient counts once it has at least 100 pounds applied across all loaded years (since 2014) and at least 90 percent of those pounds came from CDPR-flagged fumigant products or were applied by field fumigation.

    CDPR’s flag has gaps

    Telone, a soil fumigant, was re-registered in 2021 under new registration numbers that the flag does not cover, so from 2021 on it was missing from the Fumigants figures. The earlier Fumigants totals for 2021 onward were roughly 30–40 percent low. A product marked Fumigant that CDPR has not flagged says so in its badge.

    How a field fumigation was done is recorded only in some places

    Since 2008, CDPR has required the fumigation method (tarp, deep injection, drip and so on) for field fumigations in the state’s five air-quality non-attainment areas, the San Joaquin Valley among them, and each report carries a code for it. Fumigant pages show it under “How it was fumigated”. It is blank for fumigations outside those areas and before 2008, and those show as “Not recorded”; that means no code was filed, not that no method was used. The number under a technique is the section of the fumigation regulations (3 CCR 6447–6450) that governs it.

    What the units mean

    Pounds are not harm. Twenty tonnes of sulfur and twenty pounds of a fumigant are not the same exposure, and this site does not weight them. A badge says a chemical appears on an official list; it says nothing about how much of it reached anyone.

    Chemicals and commodities are pounds of active ingredient; products are pounds of product

    Pounds of active ingredient are the pesticide itself, not the mixture it was sold in. Pounds of product include everything else in the container. The two are not comparable, and this explorer never adds them together. A product leaderboard and a chemical leaderboard are measuring different things, which is why the same application can rank very differently on each.

    An application is one use report; a product with two active ingredients counts once

    Pounds of product and acre-treatments count once per report too. On a chemical’s own page each ingredient of the report counts toward that chemical, so the pages of two ingredients of the same product each show that application.

    An acre-treatment is one acre treated once

    A hundred-acre block sprayed twenty times is two thousand acre-treatments, so the figure counts applications over ground rather than ground itself and runs well past the acreage a county has. It answers how much treating happened, not how much land was treated.

    Only areas count as acres

    CDPR’s “acres treated” is reported in whatever unit the report names. Square feet are converted to acres; reports measured in cubic feet or pounds (fumigated storage or harvested crops), tons, or counts such as bait stations add no acreage, though their pounds and applications still count. The Records table shows those reports in their own unit.

    How the aggregates are built

    Every page reads a rollup

    It is summed from the raw records by year, month, county, section, product, chemical and commodity, with per-county and per-section totals derived from that. It is the same arithmetic as summing the records directly, kept ready so a page doesn't re-add millions of rows on each visit.

    Rates carry two denominators

    Pounds per square mile of a whole place, and pounds per square mile that reported any use at all. They disagree sharply wherever much of a place is unfarmed — Kern is last in the valley on the first and first on the second — so both are shown rather than a single figure that could be argued either way.

    A comparison between two years applies the same filters to both

    The change you see is the change in what you are looking at, not the section's overall change.

    Reading the maps

    Shades are relative to what is on the screen

    Squares are sorted into classes against each other, so the same color means different amounts on different maps, and a map of one county is not comparable to a map of the valley by color alone. The legend gives the actual range.

    “No data” is not “no change”

    When comparing two years, a square with nothing reported in either year is drawn as having no data; a square that reported the same amount in both is drawn as unchanged. They look different on purpose.
